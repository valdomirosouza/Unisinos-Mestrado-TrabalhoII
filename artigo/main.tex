\begin{abstract}
Incident response (IR) in modern IT operations is constrained by two
time-based indicators --- Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) and Mean Time to
Recovery (MTTR) --- and by the cognitive load imposed on operations teams
(SRE, NOC, SOC) by alert fatigue and telemetry complexity. Agentic AI
systems, which combine Large Language Models (LLMs) with planning, memory,
tool use, and human supervision, are increasingly proposed as
\emph{copilots} for these teams. This article reports a systematic
literature review (SLR), conducted under the guidelines of Kitchenham et
al.\ and the PRISMA~2020 flow, that consolidates the evidence on Agentic
AI copilots for incident response, with cognitive-load reduction treated
as a complementary outcome. Across three update cycles over a
foundational corpus, 126~candidate studies (2020--2026) were identified
and screened, 74 were assessed in full text by \emph{three independent
evaluators} (agreement 91.9--93.2\% per pair; Fleiss' $\kappa=0.60$;
66/74 unanimous decisions, with a divergence-free third cycle), and
\textbf{65 studies} were included. Three findings result. First, through
two cycles no included study measured MTTD or MTTR nominally against its
own executed baseline; the third cycle surfaces a first cluster of four
primary studies that do --- MTTD reduced by 82--83\%, MTTR by 72--91\%,
and time-to-containment by 38\% against executed human, polling, or SOAR
baselines --- but all four appear in sub-threshold venues under a
mandatory stratum caveat, with production evidence from a single vendor,
moving the headline claim from \emph{unverified} to \emph{supported by
early, low-stratum evidence}. Second, outside that cluster the evidence
remains dominated by detection proxies (accuracy, F1, AUC) and
qualitative claims, while secondary sources relay dramatic gains (e.g.,
MTTR from 8~hours to 90~minutes) that primary experiments are only
beginning to approach. Third, direct measurement of operator cognitive
load exists in adjacent domains but has never been combined with an
agentic copilot in IT incident response --- a gap all three cycles leave
untouched. These findings define a concrete agenda: venue-quality,
multi-site field evaluations of agentic copilots instrumented for
nominal MTTD/MTTR with their own baselines and validated cognitive-load
instruments.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Agentic AI, AIOps, cognitive load, incident response, LLM agents,
multi-agent systems, MTTD, MTTR, SOC, systematic literature review.
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\PARstart{M}{odern} IT operations teams face a structural asymmetry:
distributed, cloud-native systems generate telemetry at machine speed,
while detection, diagnosis, and remediation remain bounded by human
cognition. The operational cost of this asymmetry is captured by two
indicators --- \emph{Mean Time to Detect} (MTTD) and \emph{Mean Time to
Recovery} (MTTR) --- and by a human cost that is harder to see on
dashboards: alert fatigue and sustained cognitive load on SRE, NOC, and
SOC personnel. Industry reports cited in the corpus place average breach
identification and containment at 277~days (204 to detect, 73 to
contain)~\cite{P79}, while SOC analysts discard the majority of alerts as
false positives.

\emph{Agentic AI} --- AI systems that pursue goals with some degree of
autonomy by combining LLM-based reasoning with planning, memory, tool
use, and human oversight~\cite{P09,P10} --- has been proposed as a new
operating model for incident response: not full automation, but a
\emph{copilot} that perceives, reasons, acts, and learns alongside the
human operator. The promise is threefold: earlier detection (MTTD),
faster remediation (MTTR), and a lighter day-to-day workload for
operations teams.

This article asks whether the published evidence supports that promise.
It reports a systematic literature review (SLR) conducted under the
guidelines of Kitchenham et al.~\cite{kitchenham2009}, with study
selection reported in the PRISMA~2020 format~\cite{page2021prisma}, scope
delimited with the PICOC framework~\cite{petticrew2006}, and quality
assessment mirroring the DARE criteria~\cite{dare}. The review was
executed as an \emph{update review} in three cycles over a foundational
corpus of 19 studies, processing 126 candidate studies in total, and ---
unusually for SLRs in this area --- every full-text assessment was
performed independently by \emph{three evaluators}, with inter-rater
reliability quantified by Cohen's~\cite{cohen1960} and
Fleiss'~\cite{fleiss1971} kappa.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a consolidated corpus of 65 studies (2020--2026) on Agentic AI
        for incident response and adjacent IT operations, organized into
        a taxonomy of agentic paradigms (Section~\ref{sec:results});
  \item triple-verified bibliometrics (OpenAlex, Crossref, Scopus) and an
        internal cross-citation map of the corpus (65 citing edges across
        all three cycles) identifying its conceptual hubs;
  \item evidence synthesis tracking the review's central claim across
        cycles: \textbf{no nominal MTTD/MTTR measurement with an own
        baseline existed through cycle~2; a first four-study cluster
        emerges in cycle~3, entirely at sub-threshold venues}
        (Section~\ref{sec:mttr}), together with a catalog of the proxies
        otherwise used;
  \item a bridge to the human-factors literature: a cluster of studies
        that measure operator cognitive load directly --- yet never in
        combination with an agentic copilot in IT incident response
        (Section~\ref{sec:cogload});
  \item a methodological contribution: a fully reproducible three-rater
        protocol whose residual disagreements (8 of 74 decisions, all
        from the first two cycles) are traceable to three explicit
        protocol rules rather than to content interpretation --- rules
        that, once ratified and rendered into the normative prompt text,
        produced a \emph{divergence-free} third cycle
        (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{From AIOps to Agentic AI}
AIOps applies machine learning to IT operations data --- logs, metrics,
traces --- for anomaly detection, root cause analysis (RCA), and
remediation. Surveys in the corpus trace its evolution from
knowledge-based automation to ML pipelines~\cite{P16} and, after 2023,
to LLM-centered architectures~\cite{P05}. The agentic turn adds goal
pursuit under governance: taxonomies distinguish \emph{AI agents}
(tool-using, single-task) from \emph{Agentic AI} (multi-step autonomy
with planning and memory)~\cite{P09}, and reference surveys map its
technologies, applications, and societal implications~\cite{P10,P15,P54}.

\subsection{Incident response and its metrics}
Incident response spans detection, triage, diagnosis (RCA), remediation,
and post-incident learning. MTTD and MTTR are its canonical outcome
metrics; alert fatigue and operator cognitive load are its canonical
human costs. In security operations, the same cycle appears as SOC
triage and SOAR playbooks~\cite{P28,P79}. A persistent finding of this
review --- anticipated here --- is that primary studies rarely
instrument these nominal metrics, reporting detection-quality proxies
instead.

\subsection{The foundational corpus and its hubs}
The present review updates a foundational SLR (19 studies, P01--P19;
the protocol identifiers Pxx used throughout, notably in
Figs.~\ref{fig:prisma} and~\ref{fig:crosscite}, are mapped to the
bibliography in the Appendix, Table~\ref{tab:pids}).
Cross-citation analysis over the full corpus (Section~\ref{sec:cross})
shows that three foundational surveys act as conceptual hubs ---
\cite{P10} and \cite{P14} (7 internal citations each) and \cite{P09} (6)
--- confirming their role as the definitional substrate on which the
empirical studies build.

\section{Review Method}
\label{sec:method}

The protocol follows Kitchenham et al.~\cite{kitchenham2009}: planning
(PICOC, research questions, search string), conducting (search,
screening, quality assessment, extraction), and reporting (this
article). All protocol artifacts --- prompts, extraction sheets,
per-study assessments, and machine-readable result tables --- are
version-controlled in the research repository.

\subsection{PICOC}
\label{sec:picoc}

Table~\ref{tab:picoc} shows the PICOC delimitation~\cite{petticrew2006}.
A protocol rule (v1.1.0), introduced after a three-rater calibration of
the PICOC extraction itself, requires that \emph{Comparison} be recorded
as declared \textbf{only when the study executes an empirical comparison
against a concrete baseline}; purely conceptual contrasts (e.g.,
``Agentic AI vs.\ traditional AI'' in prose) are recorded as not
applicable. This rule eliminated 100\% of the inter-rater divergences
observed in the Comparison field.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Context/Definitions).} Which capabilities and
        autonomy levels (planning, memory, tool use, human supervision)
        characterize Agentic AI, and how do they affect decision-making
        in complex environments?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Engineering/Architecture).} Which architectures and
        mechanisms (orchestration, memory, tools, guardrails,
        observability) sustain Agentic AI in production?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (Evidence/Benefits).} Which quantitative and
        qualitative evidence reports benefits, and which metrics are
        used (response time, decision quality, operational load),
        especially in incident response?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (Challenges/Ethics).} Which risks and challenges
        (security, robustness, governance, accountability) limit
        responsible use in critical systems?
  \item \textbf{RQ5 (Gaps).} Which gaps and future directions
        (evaluation/benchmarking, threat models, governance,
        observability, alignment) are identified?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Search and identification}
The search string was derived from the cross-study PICOC synthesis as
two mandatory blocks --- \emph{Intervention} (agentic AI, LLM agents,
multi-agent systems, AIOps copilots) AND \emph{Context/Population}
(incident response, IT operations, SRE/NOC/SOC, observability) ---
with \emph{Outcomes} deliberately optional, since only 1 of 38 calibration
studies named MTTD/MTTR in its metadata. The string was calibrated
against the known corpus and validated by real execution on OpenAlex
($\approx$49{,}700 records) and on the Scopus Search API (12{,}783
records, 2020+, English), achieving 13/14 recall of the included
calibration set in both engines with the same single miss. Candidate
identification combined assisted search (cycle~1: three independent LLM
assistants, $\approx$51 raw records), author-driven discovery (cycle~2:
49 candidates), and re-execution of the validated query in five database
sweeps (cycle~3: 37 new candidates). Backward/forward snowballing is
mandatory in the protocol because one included study is not indexed by
Scopus and one is not retrieved by the main query; the cycle-3
snowballing pass (backward over OpenAlex-deposited references plus a
full-text scan of the entire PDF archive; forward over the citers of the
one high-citation candidate excluded by the series-stratum rule) yielded
no additional eligible candidate.

\subsection{Selection criteria}
Inclusion required: publication year $\geq$2020 (I1); an identifiable
peer-reviewed venue (I2); venue quality thresholds (I3--I4, national
Qualis A1--A2 and SJR Q1--Q2); at least one citation in any of three
indexers, with a recency exception for papers younger than 12~months
(I5); and thematic adherence --- an \emph{agentic} approach in the
\emph{IR/AIOps/SOC} domain (I6). Exclusion criteria: sub-threshold venue
strata (E1), non-agentic approaches (E2), out-of-domain studies (E3),
duplicates (E4), and non-peer-reviewed material (E5). A dated protocol
amendment in cycle~2 allowed four candidates outside the I3/I4 strata to
proceed to full-text assessment \emph{with a mandatory stratum caveat};
the amendment is itself part of the audit trail and, notably, is the
root cause of three of the eight residual inter-rater divergences
(Section~\ref{sec:reliability}).

Cycle~3 brought the review's first conference papers (26 of 37
candidates), for which the journal-oriented I3/I4 strata are undefined.
Two further dated decisions were ratified before screening: the stratum
of a conference paper is derived from the \emph{Scopus CiteScore
percentile of its proceedings series} mapped through the same
percentile-to-stratum scale used for journals, with series lacking a
CiteScore ineligible at screening; and the stratum amendment was renewed
for the cycle, so sub-threshold candidates again proceeded with the
mandatory caveat. Finally, five accepted candidates (Springer proceedings
chapters) whose full texts could not be obtained through the
institutional subscription after two documented attempts were removed
under the PRISMA~2020 ``reports not retrieved'' category --- an exclusion
by unavailability, not by merit.

\subsection{Ratified protocol rules (R1--R3)}
\label{sec:rules}

The inter-rater analysis (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}) traced every
residual evaluator divergence to three recurring boundary questions.
After that analysis, the review conductor ratified the following rules,
which are consistent with --- and now normatively bind --- the published
corpus decisions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{R1 (non-agentic secondary studies).} Secondary studies
        (surveys/SLRs) whose object is \emph{not} Agentic AI are
        excluded from the primary corpus regardless of methodological
        quality; agentic-centered secondary studies are retained as
        \emph{foundational-conditional} background
        (e.g., \cite{P24,P33,P52,P54,P79}). This ratifies the exclusion
        of the non-agentic RCA and log-anomaly reviews assessed in
        cycle~1.
  \item \textbf{R2 (normative placement of amendments).} Eligibility
        amendments bind only when written into the \emph{normative
        Stage-1 text} of the assessment protocol, not into its input
        metadata. The cycle-2 stratum amendment is ratified under this
        rule: the four sub-stratum candidates keep their full
        assessments with a mandatory stratum caveat attached to any use
        of their evidence.
  \item \textbf{R3 (domain boundary and the human-factors cluster).}
        Out-of-domain studies are excluded from the primary corpus even
        when methodologically strong; the human-factors cluster
        \cite{P62,P63,P69,P81} is retained strictly as
        \emph{complementary evidence for the cognitive-load outcome}
        (Table~\ref{tab:cogload}) and never feeds the agentic research
        questions (RQ1, RQ2, RQ4); a cluster study with no RQ adherence
        at all remains excluded.
\end{itemize}

Cycle~3 was conducted \emph{prospectively} under R1--R3: the ratified
rules (including all active amendments) were rendered into the normative
Stage-1 text of every assessment prompt from a single canonical
template. The outcome is reported in Section~\ref{sec:reliability}: the
cycle produced \emph{zero} inter-rater divergences, which we read as an
empirical verification of the rules --- in particular of R2, since the
evaluator that had halted on sub-stratum candidates in cycle~2 assessed
all of them in cycle~3 once the amendment was normative text.

\subsection{Quality assessment and extraction}
Each assessed study received: (i)~a verified bibliometric record
(DOI, venue, year, and citation counts kept \emph{per source} ---
OpenAlex, Crossref, Scopus --- never summed, with ``not indexed''
distinguished from zero); (ii)~an RQ-coverage score (RQ1--RQ5, each
rated fully/partially/not answered = 1.0/0.5/0.0, max 5.0); (iii)~a
DARE-style quality score (QA1 clear objectives, QA2 replicable
methodology, QA3 solid evidence base, QA4 coherent conclusions; max
4.0; bands High $\geq$3.0, Medium 1.5--2.5, Low $<$1.5); and (iv)~an
11-field structured extraction sheet with per-claim evidence anchors
(section/page). The 19 foundational studies were re-assessed
retroactively under the same rubric --- without re-opening their
inherited inclusion --- so that all 74 assessments are directly
comparable.

\subsection{Three-evaluator protocol}
Every full-text assessment was executed independently by three LLM-based
evaluators (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini) from the same written prompt and
PDF. The agreement set comprises 74 decisions: the 19 foundational
re-assessments, all 20 screened cycle-1 candidates (the two
stratum-ineligible candidates also received full assessments, recorded
as exclusions), the 22 cycle-2 assessments, and the 13 cycle-3
assessments. Agreement was computed
on collapsed include/exclude decisions with
Cohen's $\kappa$ per pair~\cite{cohen1960} and Fleiss' $\kappa$ overall
\cite{fleiss1971}. Results are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:reliability}.

\subsection{RQ coverage and quality}
Fig.~\ref{fig:rqcov} shows per-RQ coverage across the four study sets.
RQ coverage shifted between cycles in a telling way. In cycle~1
(18 assessed; mean RQ score 3.83, mean QA 3.50) the systematic gap was

cycle~2 (22 assessed; mean RQ 3.16, mean QA 2.73; zero unconditional
inclusions) the gap moved to \textbf{RQ3/QA3}: abundant conceptual
contribution with scarce empirical benefit evidence. The retroactive
assessment of the foundational studies (mean RQ 3.66, mean QA 2.53)

however, remains middling, dominated by testbed and synthetic evidence.

literature is conceptually rich and empirically thin precisely on the
benefit claims that motivate it} --- and cycle~3 is the first measurable
bend in that curve, at sub-threshold venues.

\subsection{Evidence on MTTD and MTTR}
\label{sec:mttr}

This is the review's central result, and the one the third cycle
materially changes. Through the first two cycles (89 candidates
screened, 61 assessed in full text), \textbf{no study measured MTTD or
MTTR nominally against its own executed baseline}: the single nominal
MTTR datapoint --- the SOC hyper-automation agent of~\cite{P76},
$\approx$1--1.5~min per phase in a proof of concept compared against
\emph{literature values} for a commercial copilot ($\approx$30--45~s)
--- had no baseline of its own and was unfavorable to the proposed
agent, while secondary sources relayed dramatic but unreproduced gains
(MTTR from 8~hours to 90~minutes; 98\% auto-triage with sub-5-minute
containment; a 277-day industry detect-and-contain
baseline)~\cite{P79}.

Cycle~3 surfaces the corpus's \textbf{first cluster of primary studies
with nominal time metrics against executed baselines}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Production, human baseline.} The agentic SRE copilot
        of~\cite{P100} (five LLM agents over a knowledge graph, with a
        guarded action layer) reports production p90 MTTD falling from
        30 to 5~minutes ($-83\%$) and p90 MTTR from 240 to 20~minutes
        ($-91\%$; $\approx$$-63\%$ in the paper's aggregate figure)
        against experienced human SREs with dashboards and runbooks,
        at a reported LLM cost of \$0.079 per incident.
  \item \textbf{Testbed, ablated baselines.} The event-driven SOC
        with a supervisor agent of~\cite{P101} reports MTTR of 71.7~s
        vs.\ 258.4~s for a polling baseline ($-72.3\%$) and 190.4~s for
        an LLM-assist (advisory-only) ablation ($-62.3\%$), over 1{,}247
        synthetic events drawn from MITRE ATT\&CK scenarios. The multi-agent SRE framework
        of~\cite{P102} reports MTTD of 8.5 vs.\ 47.2~minutes ($-82\%$)
        against a consensus baseline of three senior SREs --- MTTR
        itself is not measured.
  \item \textbf{Public dataset, internal baseline.} The adaptive
        counterfactual-validation framework of~\cite{P123} reports mean
        time-to-containment of 8.9 vs.\ 14.3~minutes ($-37.8\%$,
        Wilcoxon $p<0.001$) against a SOAR baseline executed by the
        authors on the same 143 DARPA OpTC alerts; its external
        baselines are literature-derived and explicitly indicative only.
\end{itemize}

Three qualifications bound this cluster, and they are the reason the
review's conclusion changes in degree rather than in kind. First,
\emph{venue stratum}: all four studies appear in sub-threshold venues
(proceedings series at the B2/B4 percentile bands or journals below the
A1--A2 bar) and therefore carry the mandatory stratum caveat --- at the
venue-quality bar of criteria I3/I4, the nominal-measurement gap is
formally intact. Second, \emph{setting}: only \cite{P100} is a
production deployment, and it is a single, author-affiliated one;
\cite{P101,P102} are testbeds and \cite{P93} (which reports MTTD/MTTR
reductions of 44--80\% for an agentic SOC against a traditional-SOC arm)
runs both arms on synthetic telemetry with internally inconsistent
tables, recorded in its assessment. Third, \emph{independence}: the
three evaluators unanimously included all four studies, always with the
caveat attached. Outside this cluster, proxies still dominate: a
74--84\% alert-volume reduction in a national SOC~\cite{P73}, operator
reaction time falling from $\approx$276~s to $\approx$107~s with
decision support~\cite{P81}, report latency of
2.9--11.6~s~\cite{P87}, and answer quality without
timing~\cite{P83,P119}; detection studies report accuracy/F1/AUC only.

The structural mismatch diagnosed after cycle~2 --- a field whose
motivating claim is time-metric reduction but whose measurement practice
is detection proxies --- is therefore beginning to close, from the
low-stratum end of the publication spectrum first. As of late 2026, the
claim that agentic copilots reduce MTTD and MTTR is \emph{supported by
early, low-stratum primary evidence and remains unverified at this
review's venue-quality bar}.

\subsection{Cognitive load: the complementary outcome}
\label{sec:cogload}

The second gap concerns an outcome this review treats as complementary
rather than incidental: operator cognitive load. The motivating
observation was already present in the foundational review --- the
explosion of observability data (metrics, logs, traces) has raised the
cognitive load of operations teams faster than tooling has relieved
it~\cite{P05,P16} --- yet, within IT incident response, \emph{no corpus
study measures cognitive load directly}; alert fatigue appears only as
an alert-volume proxy~\cite{P73}. Direct measurement does exist in
adjacent domains, and cycle~2 deliberately retained this human-factors
cluster (with stratum caveats under the protocol amendment):
neuroergonomic foundations of mental workload and
engagement~\cite{P62}; a multimodal workload database with
classification AUC up to 0.998 and NASA-TLX ground truth~\cite{P69};
and a human experiment in which an AI decision-support system improved
control-room operator performance~\cite{P81}. The corpus also offers a
validated usability instrument for conversational agents
(BUS-15)~\cite{P63}. Cycle~3 does not change this picture: its
time-instrumented cluster measures system latencies, not operator state,
and its one human-facing evaluation scores answer quality with
LLM-as-judge rubrics rather than workload~\cite{P119}.

Table~\ref{tab:cogload} consolidates these assets into a
\emph{measurement toolbox} for future copilot studies --- in our view,
the most reusable contribution this corpus makes to the cognitive-load
question. The decisive observation is that the intersection
\emph{agentic copilot $\times$ IT incident response $\times$ measured
cognitive load} is empty: every instrument in
Table~\ref{tab:cogload} has been validated, but never in the presence
of an agentic copilot in IT operations. Together with
Section~\ref{sec:mttr}, this defines the experimental agenda that
motivates the authors' ongoing work: a copilot evaluated in the field
with nominal MTTD/MTTR against its own baseline \emph{and} the
instruments of Table~\ref{tab:cogload}.

\subsection{Cross-citation structure of the corpus}
\label{sec:cross}

Internal citation analysis over the full corpus (through cycle~2:
triple-checked across OpenAlex \texttt{referenced\_works}, Crossref
reference lists, and Scopus \texttt{REF()} by title; cycle-3 additions:
union of a full-text reference scan of each included PDF and
OpenAlex-deposited references; 65 citing edges in total) shows a
coherent field rather than a disconnected collection of papers. The hubs are the definitional
surveys --- \cite{P10} and \cite{P14} (7 internal citations each),
\cite{P09} (6) --- followed by the AIOps/MLOps and log-anomaly surveys
\cite{P16,P65} and the SOC hyper-automation study \cite{P76} (3 each).
The most connected citers are the multi-agent SOC system of~\cite{P28}
(7 outgoing edges, five of them to cycle-2 candidates) and the cycle-3
SOC-as-a-service framework of~\cite{P97} (7 outgoing edges, all into
earlier cycles or intra-cycle), and each cycle's studies cite back into
the corpus (\cite{P79}$\rightarrow$\cite{P20,P78};
\cite{P76}$\rightarrow$\cite{P39,P83};
\cite{P93}$\rightarrow$\cite{P06,P76}), indicating that the cycles
sample one evolving conversation. Source-specific quirks are material
to reproducibility: Scopus resolves references by \emph{title}, not
DOI; one open-access venue is not indexed there at all; and preprint
DOIs can split citation identity across sources (handled here by alias
resolution). Fig.~\ref{fig:crosscite} renders the full network. The
cycle-3 inclusions add 13 edges and deepen the pattern: the
SOC-as-a-service framework \cite{P97} cites seven corpus studies ---
among them \cite{P14,P73,P76,P79} and the intra-cycle survey \cite{P121}
--- tying the multi-agent SOC system \cite{P28} as the most connected
citer; the agentic-SOC evaluation \cite{P93} cites \cite{P06,P76}; and
the cycle-3 surveys cite both the definitional layer and cycle-2 studies
\cite{P101,P112,P121}. Two consequences for the hub structure: the SOC
hyper-automation study \cite{P76}, cited by \cite{P28,P93,P97}, becomes
a hub, and \cite{P14} rises to 7 internal citations, level with
\cite{P10}.

\subsection{Inter-rater reliability}
\label{sec:reliability}

Table~\ref{tab:kappa} reports agreement over the 74 collapsed
include/exclude decisions. Pairwise agreement is 91.9--93.2\%
($\kappa$ 0.58--0.62, with the strictest pair reaching the
\emph{substantial} band); Fleiss' $\kappa=0.60$; \textbf{66/74 decisions
are unanimous} (62 inclusions and 4 exclusions), including all 19
foundational re-assessments and the entire third cycle. The $\kappa$
values are deflated by prevalence (90.1\% of all verdicts are
``include''), so the substantive reading is the nature of the eight
divergences: \emph{all eight are protocol-rule divergences, none is a
content divergence, and all eight predate cycle~3} --- in every case the
three evaluators' scores agree and only the consequence rule differs.
They fall into exactly three families: (A)~the treatment of non-agentic
secondary studies (2 cases); (B)~whether the cycle-2 stratum amendment
binds, which one evaluator --- applying the strict criterion --- did
not honor (3 cases); and (C)~domain/stratum boundary calls (3 cases).
These three families were ratified as protocol rules R1--R3
(Section~\ref{sec:rules}) and cycle~3 was run prospectively under them:
its 13 assessments came out \textbf{unanimous 13/13} ($\kappa=1.00$ in
every pair within the cycle), including the unanimous thematic exclusion
of the one screening-flagged borderline study --- an empirical
verification that the rules, once normative, eliminate the divergence
classes they name. Mean absolute score differences remain small
(RQ 0.43--0.61; QA 0.31--0.61), with stable evaluator profiles:
one strict on protocol and quality, one lenient on scores (though its
quality bands matched the intermediate evaluator's exactly in cycle~3),
one intermediate with the hardest thematic cut.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the evidence supports --- and what it does not}
The corpus firmly supports three claims: (i)~a consolidated conceptual
model of Agentic AI for operations (autonomy levels, orchestration,
guardrails, HITL) \cite{P09,P10,P15,P54,P112}; (ii)~technical
feasibility of agentic RCA, triage, and remediation at prototype level,
with strong detection-quality results
\cite{P21,P22,P27,P28,P31,P35,P102}; and (iii)~a well-articulated risk
and governance agenda \cite{P01,P12,P17,P14,P94}. The headline
operational claim now has its first primary support --- the cycle-3
cluster of Section~\ref{sec:mttr} --- but that support is early,
concentrated at sub-threshold venues, and production-grade in exactly
one author-affiliated deployment. Reviewers and practitioners should
still treat vendor-scale numbers (e.g., 8h $\rightarrow$ 90min MTTR) as
hypotheses under test: the cycle-3 measurements are the first tests, not
yet the established effect.

\subsection{Why the measurement gap persisted --- and where it is closing}
Three causes were visible through cycle~2: production access (most
studies evaluate on public datasets or testbeds; industrial deployments
are rare and rarely instrumented for time metrics); benchmark culture
(detection communities optimize F1/AUC, which do not compose into
end-to-end time outcomes); and the autonomy--accountability tension
(studies that deploy agents constrain them to low-risk actions, making
time gains modest and hard to attribute). That the only pre-cycle-3
nominal measurement~\cite{P76} was \emph{unfavorable} to the agent
remains a useful counterweight to publication bias. Cycle~3 adds a
fourth, sociological observation: the first time-instrumented studies
are appearing in early-stage venues --- regional conference series and
recently launched journals --- ahead of the A-stratum literature. A
review that enforced its venue bar without the caveat mechanism would
have reported the measurement gap as fully intact; the dated-amendment
design is what makes the field's actual turning point visible while
keeping the evidence grade explicit.

\subsection{Research gaps consolidated across the reviews}
\label{sec:gapaxes}
The foundational review synthesized six gap axes for agentic IR; the
three update cycles allow each to be re-examined against newer evidence.
\textbf{(i)~From recommendation to safe autonomous execution.}
Remediation in LLM4AIOps was found to stop at recommendation and
triage~\cite{P05,P15}; cycles 1--2 confirmed the axis --- closed-loop
execution at prototype level~\cite{P22,P32} and at proof-of-concept
level in SOC hyper-automation~\cite{P76} --- and cycle~3 moves it
furthest: guarded production execution with human gates~\cite{P100},
event-driven SOAR tool-calling~\cite{P101}, and counterfactual
pre-execution validation with adaptive re-planning~\cite{P123}.
Verifiable, auditable execution beyond a single vendor deployment
remains unreported.
\textbf{(ii)~Complex telemetry (traces).} The difficulty of feeding
distributed traces to LLMs~\cite{P05} persists: cycle-2 primary studies
operate on logs, alerts, and flows~\cite{P53,P73,P87}; trace-native
agentic RCA remains open.
\textbf{(iii)~Memory integrity and contradictory knowledge.}
Memory poisoning and conflicting sources were flagged as structural
risks~\cite{P01}; no update-cycle study evaluates agent-memory integrity
empirically --- the axis is untouched.
\textbf{(iv)~Governance, accountability, and adversarial risk.}
The richest axis in volume: governance frameworks and threat
taxonomies~\cite{P01,P12,P14,P17,P72,P77} and HITL adoption
factors~\cite{P08,P37}, yet still without quantitative evaluation of
governance mechanisms under incident conditions.
\textbf{(v)~Causal reasoning vs.\ statistical correlation.}
Agentic RCA advanced with graph- and Bayesian-structured
approaches~\cite{P32,P35,P53}, partially addressing the axis, though
evaluation remains on curated failure sets.
\textbf{(vi)~Resource efficiency and sustainability.}
The SLM agent of~\cite{P21} is still the corpus's only direct response
(compact models, confidentiality, cost); the axis otherwise remains a
statement of intent. Axes (i), (iii), and (vi) are therefore the least
served by three years of literature --- a concrete map for future
primary studies, to which this review adds a seventh axis:
\emph{measured cognitive-load outcomes}
(Section~\ref{sec:cogload}).

\subsection{Implications for research and practice}
For researchers, the agenda is concrete: replicate the cycle-3 cluster
at this review's venue-quality bar --- field studies that instrument
MTTD and MTTR nominally, pre/post or A/B against the team's own
baseline, multi-site rather than single-vendor
\cite{P100,P101,P102,P123} --- and pair time metrics with validated
human-factors instruments (NASA-TLX~\cite{P69}, BUS-15~\cite{P63}) to
test the cognitive-load ``bonus'' that no study has yet measured. For
practitioners, the corpus supports piloting copilots on triage and
report drafting (where latency and quality evidence exists
\cite{P83,P87,P119}) under HITL governance \cite{P08,P37}, now with
early quantitative guidance on attainable time reductions and their
cost~\cite{P100,P101}, while treating full-loop autonomous remediation
as experimental \cite{P22,P76,P123} and securing the agent pipeline
itself \cite{P94}.

\subsection{Methodological lessons for LLM-assisted SLRs}
Three findings generalize beyond this topic. First, three independent
LLM evaluators under one written protocol achieve high percentage
agreement (91.9--93.2\% pairwise) with moderate-to-substantial
chance-corrected $\kappa$, and their residual disagreements expose
\emph{protocol ambiguities, not model randomness} --- a cheap and
effective audit mechanism. Second, protocol amendments must be placed in
the normative body of the evaluation prompt, not in its input metadata:
the one evaluator that missed the cycle-2 stratum amendment read it as
advisory, producing three of the eight divergences. Third, the fix is
verifiable: once the ratified rules were rendered into the normative
prompt text from a canonical template, the next full cycle (13
assessments, including sub-stratum candidates and a flagged borderline
exclusion) ran \emph{divergence-free} --- protocol maturation in this
design is observable as a measured drop in disagreement, not merely
asserted.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Selection.} Assisted identification (LLM search in cycle~1,
author-driven discovery in cycle~2, query re-execution in cycle~3) may
bias candidate coverage; this is mitigated by dual-engine string
validation (13/14 recall on OpenAlex and Scopus), mandatory snowballing
(executed in cycle~3 with a negative result), and full audit trails, but
a database-exhaustive census was not the goal of the update design. Five
cycle-3 candidates were removed as PRISMA ``reports not retrieved''
after two documented access attempts --- an unavailability exclusion
whose effect on the synthesis is unknowable by construction.
\textbf{Strata criteria.} Venue-quality thresholds (I3--I4) use a
national classification (Qualis) alongside SJR, extended to proceedings
series by Scopus CiteScore percentile in cycle~3; the dated amendments
that retained sub-threshold studies are explicit. The pre-cycle-3
conclusions of Sections~\ref{sec:mttr}--\ref{sec:cogload} hold with the
caveated studies removed; the cycle-3 nominal-metric cluster, by
contrast, exists \emph{only} under the caveat --- removing sub-threshold
studies removes the cluster, which is precisely why its evidence grade
is reported alongside its effect sizes, and why one of its four studies
is additionally an author-affiliated single-vendor deployment.
\textbf{Evaluator validity.} All three raters are LLM systems;
they are independent of each other but share potential model-family
biases. The unanimity of the core corpus (89\%), the protocol-only
nature of divergences, per-claim evidence anchors in every assessment,
and a divergence-free third cycle under the ratified rules mitigate ---
but do not eliminate --- this threat; the three protocol questions
exposed by the divergences were adjudicated by the (human) review
conductor and ratified as rules R1--R3 (Section~\ref{sec:rules}).
\textbf{Bibliometric sources.} Citation counts are kept per source and
never summed; source quirks (title-based reference resolution, unindexed
venues, preprint DOI splits, API service-level limits) are documented in
the audit trail.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This SLR consolidated 65 studies (2020--2026) on Agentic AI as a copilot
for incident response across three update cycles, assessed by three
independent evaluators with high agreement (66 of 74 decisions
unanimous; the third cycle divergence-free). The field has matured
conceptually --- shared definitions, reference architectures, governance
agendas, and a coherent citation structure --- and demonstrates
prototype-level feasibility of agentic detection, RCA, and remediation.
Its motivating claim is now beginning to be measured: a first cluster of
four primary studies reports nominal MTTD, MTTR, and time-to-containment
reductions of 38--91\% against executed baselines --- but entirely at
sub-threshold venues, with production evidence from a single
author-affiliated deployment, and still without any measurement of
operator cognitive load in the presence of an agentic copilot. The most
valuable next contribution to this literature is therefore a field
evaluation that clears the bar the early evidence does not: an agentic
copilot deployed with operations teams beyond a single vendor,
instrumented for MTTD/MTTR with its own baseline, paired with validated
workload instruments, and reported at a venue-quality level that lets
systematic reviews count it without caveat. That study is the direct
continuation of this review.
